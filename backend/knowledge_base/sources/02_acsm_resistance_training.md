# ACSM Resistance Training Guidelines

## Overview of ACSM Resistance Training Recommendations

The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) publishes evidence-based guidelines for resistance training that are the gold standard for clinical and recreational exercise programming. The 2009 and 2022 position stands synthesise hundreds of peer-reviewed studies to provide specific prescriptions for load, volume, rest, and frequency across fitness levels and training goals. These guidelines are designed to be adaptable to any equipment environment and to minimise injury risk while maximising adaptation.

## Rep Ranges and Their Physiological Effects

The relationship between repetitions performed per set and the primary physiological adaptation is well established:

**1–5 reps (≥85% 1RM)**: Primary adaptation is maximal strength through neural efficiency and myofibrillar density. Motor unit recruitment is near-maximal, and the mechanical tension on muscle fibres is highest. This range is recommended for strength-focused goals and advanced trainees.

**6–12 reps (67–85% 1RM)**: The primary hypertrophy range, producing the greatest combination of mechanical tension, metabolic stress, and muscle damage — the three primary drivers of muscle growth. ACSM recommends this range for intermediate and advanced trainees targeting size.

**12–20 reps (50–67% 1RM)**: Primarily builds muscular endurance with meaningful hypertrophy at the lower end of the range. Higher rep sets at moderate loads also produce significant metabolic adaptation and are appropriate for beginners, circuit training, and endurance-focused programming.

**20+ reps (<50% 1RM)**: Predominantly trains muscular endurance and cardiovascular conditioning. High-rep sets with light loads produce minimal strength or hypertrophy gains but are appropriate for rehabilitation, warm-up sets, and metabolic conditioning blocks.

Importantly, research (Schoenfeld et al., 2017) demonstrates that load-equated sets produce similar hypertrophy across a wide rep range (6–30 reps) as long as sets are taken close to failure. The rep range primarily determines the load used, not the magnitude of hypertrophy.

## Set Volume Recommendations by Goal and Level

ACSM's volume recommendations in sets per muscle group per week:

**Beginners (0–12 months)**:
- 1–3 sets per exercise, 2–3 exercises per major muscle group per week
- Whole-body or upper/lower splits twice a week are most effective at this stage
- Focus on learning movement patterns; volume is secondary to technique

**Intermediate (1–3 years)**:
- 3–6 sets per exercise, 10–20 total sets per muscle group per week
- An upper/lower or push/pull/legs split 3–4 days per week is typical

**Advanced (3+ years)**:
- 4–6 sets per exercise, 16–22+ sets per muscle group per week
- May require higher frequencies (3–4× per week per muscle) to distribute volume
- Volume must be periodised to prevent accumulation of excessive fatigue

These are weekly targets, not per-session targets. Spreading volume across multiple sessions is more effective for muscle protein synthesis than cramming all sets into one long session.

## Rest Periods Between Sets

Rest period length affects recovery completeness and training adaptations:

**2–5 minutes**: Recommended for maximal strength work (1–5 reps, heavy loads). Allows near-complete ATP-PCr restoration and neuromuscular recovery, enabling high quality subsequent sets.

**60–90 seconds**: Commonly used for hypertrophy training (6–12 reps). Creates metabolic stress and growth hormone release while preserving enough recovery to complete subsequent sets with meaningful load.

**30–60 seconds**: Muscular endurance and circuit training. Increases cardiovascular demand and caloric expenditure but limits the load that can be used on subsequent sets.

Recent research (Schoenfeld et al., 2016) shows that longer rest periods (2–3 minutes) between hypertrophy-range sets produce greater volume load and more hypertrophy than short rest (1 minute), challenging the traditional recommendation of 60–90 seconds for muscle growth.

## Exercise Order Within a Session

ACSM recommends the following exercise sequencing principles:

1. **Large before small muscle groups**: Multi-joint exercises (squat, deadlift, bench press, row) before isolation exercises (curls, extensions, lateral raises). Compound movements require full neural and muscular output; if performed after isolation exercises, their quality is compromised.

2. **Higher-intensity before lower-intensity**: Exercises requiring maximum force production (Olympic lifts, heavy compound movements) are performed first when neuromuscular fatigue is minimal.

3. **Alternate opposing muscle groups**: Supersetting antagonists (e.g., bicep curl followed immediately by tricep extension) reduces overall session time without compromising performance.

4. **Power before strength before endurance**: In a single session targeting multiple qualities, power/explosive work is performed first, followed by strength work, then endurance/metabolic work.

## Resistance Training Frequency

**Beginners**: 2–3 days per week, full-body each session. Research shows novice trainees benefit from higher frequency of practice per movement pattern to accelerate neuromuscular learning.

**Intermediate**: 3–4 days per week. Upper/lower splits or push/pull/legs allow higher per-session volume while maintaining adequate recovery.

**Advanced**: 4–6 days per week. Each muscle group or movement pattern may be trained 2–3 times per week. Frequency is increased to distribute higher weekly volume while keeping per-session volume manageable.

A muscle group needs a minimum of 48 hours to recover from resistance training before being trained again at the same intensity. For heavy compound movements involving the central nervous system (squat, deadlift), 72 hours or more is often appropriate between high-intensity sessions.

## Resistance Training for Special Populations

**Older adults (65+)**: ACSM recommends resistance training 2–3 days per week with emphasis on functional movements (squat patterns, pushing, pulling). Loads in the 10–15 rep range are safer for untrained older adults than low-rep, maximal-load work. The benefits — sarcopenia prevention, bone density maintenance, fall risk reduction — are significant and well-documented.

**Cardiovascular disease**: With medical clearance, resistance training is recommended for most individuals with stable cardiovascular disease. Moderate intensity (40–60% 1RM), higher reps (12–15), and circuit-style training reduce peak cardiovascular demand while maintaining the musculoskeletal benefits.

**Obesity**: Resistance training is particularly important for individuals with obesity to preserve lean mass during caloric deficit. Higher-rep, multi-set protocols combined with adequate protein intake (1.6–2.2 g/kg body weight) minimise muscle loss during fat-loss phases.

## Periodisation of Resistance Training

ACSM endorses periodised programming over non-periodised training for intermediate and advanced trainees. Periodisation systematically varies training variables (volume, intensity, exercise selection) over time to prevent accommodation, manage fatigue, and peak performance.

**Linear periodisation**: Progressive increase in load, progressive decrease in volume over a training cycle. Classic approach for strength development.

**Undulating periodisation**: Daily or weekly variation of rep ranges and loads within the same training cycle. Allows multiple qualities (strength, hypertrophy, endurance) to be trained simultaneously.

**Block periodisation**: Sequential emphasis phases (accumulation → intensification → realisation) each lasting 2–6 weeks, with distinct training objectives per phase. Most appropriate for advanced trainees.

## Warm-Up for Resistance Training

ACSM recommends a structured warm-up before resistance training:

1. **General warm-up (5–10 minutes)**: Low-intensity aerobic activity (rowing, cycling, light jogging) elevates core temperature, increases blood flow to working muscles, and enhances neural activation.

2. **Dynamic mobility work (5 minutes)**: Joint circles, hip hinges, thoracic rotations specific to the movements in that day's session.

3. **Exercise-specific warm-up sets**: 2–4 progressively loaded warm-up sets at 40–60% of working weight before the first heavy set of each major exercise. This activates the specific neuromuscular patterns and primes the connective tissue.

Static stretching immediately before resistance training may reduce force production acutely and is not recommended pre-session. It is appropriate post-session as part of cool-down.
