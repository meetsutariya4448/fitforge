# Exercise Selection by Equipment

## No Equipment — Bodyweight Training

Bodyweight training is a complete and legitimate training modality for all fitness levels. The key to progressive overload without external load is manipulating leverage, range of motion, tempo, and unilateral loading.

**Primary bodyweight exercises:**

*Pushing*: Push-up (standard, wide, narrow, decline, pike, diamond), dip (using chair or parallel bars), pike push-up (vertical pressing pattern for shoulders), archer push-up (unilateral horizontal press progression).

*Pulling*: Inverted row (using a table or low bar), Australian pull-up, bodyweight row. Without a bar, pulling patterns are limited.

*Squatting*: Bodyweight squat, jump squat, sumo squat, Bulgarian split squat (rear foot elevated), pistol squat (single-leg — advanced), step-up, lunge (forward, reverse, lateral).

*Hinging*: Single-leg Romanian deadlift, glute bridge, hip thrust (both feet or single leg), Nordic hamstring curl (advanced).

*Core*: Plank (forearm and extended arm), side plank, hollow body hold, dead bug, mountain climber, leg raise, bicycle crunch, ab wheel rollout.

**Progressive overload without weights**: Increase reps, increase sets, reduce rest, add unilateral variations (one arm/one leg), add instability, increase range of motion (deficit push-ups, deep squats), or add tempo (3-second eccentric, 1-second pause at bottom).

**Appropriate for**: All fitness levels; beginners build a strength foundation; advanced trainees use weighted progressions or skill-based variations (handstand push-up, planche progressions).

## Dumbbells

Dumbbells allow unilateral training (each arm/leg works independently), which addresses muscular imbalances and increases core stabilisation demand. They are the most versatile equipment for home gym settings.

**Primary dumbbell exercises:**

*Chest*: Dumbbell bench press (flat, incline 30–45°, decline), dumbbell fly (flat, incline), push-up with dumbbells (allows greater depth).

*Back*: Single-arm dumbbell row, chest-supported row, dumbbell pullover, dumbbell deadlift (Romanian, conventional).

*Shoulders*: Dumbbell overhead press (seated or standing), lateral raise, front raise, rear delt fly (bent-over), Arnold press (rotational overhead press).

*Arms*: Dumbbell curl (standard, hammer, incline, concentration, Zottman), tricep kickback, overhead tricep extension, skull crusher.

*Legs*: Goblet squat, dumbbell lunges (all variations), dumbbell Romanian deadlift, dumbbell step-up, dumbbell Bulgarian split squat, dumbbell hip thrust, dumbbell sumo squat.

*Core*: Dumbbell Russian twist, dumbbell side bend, dumbbell plank row.

**Key advantages**: Unilateral training exposes and corrects side-to-side strength imbalances. Greater ROM than barbells on many exercises (e.g., dumbbell bench press allows elbows to drop below chest level). Easier wrist positioning for those with wrist pain during barbell pressing.

**Appropriate for**: All fitness levels from beginner (light dumbbells for learning movement patterns) to advanced (heavy dumbbells for progressively loaded compound movements).

## Barbell

The barbell enables the heaviest loading of the major movement patterns and is the primary tool for developing maximal strength. Barbell training requires learning proper technique to lift safely, particularly for squat, deadlift, and overhead press.

**Primary barbell exercises:**

*Squat pattern*: Back squat (high bar, low bar), front squat, overhead squat, box squat, pause squat, tempo squat.

*Hip hinge*: Conventional deadlift, sumo deadlift, Romanian deadlift (RDL), good morning, stiff-leg deadlift, hip thrust.

*Horizontal push*: Barbell bench press (flat, incline, decline), close-grip bench press.

*Vertical push*: Overhead press (standing, seated), push press, behind-the-neck press (experienced lifters only).

*Horizontal pull*: Barbell row (overhand/underhand), pendlay row, yates row.

*Olympic lifts*: Power clean, hang clean, snatch, power snatch — require coaching for safe execution.

**The Big 5 for barbell beginners**: Squat, deadlift, bench press, overhead press, barbell row. These five compound movements, performed with progressive overload, are sufficient to build full-body strength from beginner to intermediate level.

**Safety considerations**: Barbell squats and bench press should always be performed in a power rack with safety bars set at the appropriate height, or with a spotter. Deadlifts require a flat, non-compressive surface.

## Resistance Bands

Resistance bands provide variable resistance — the force increases as the band is stretched. This matches the strength curve of many exercises (e.g., hip extension becomes hardest at lockout, matching peak band tension). Bands are portable, low-cost, and appropriate for all fitness levels.

**Primary resistance band exercises:**

*Upper body*: Band pull-apart (rear delts, external rotators — critical for shoulder health), face pull, band overhead press, band curl, band tricep pushdown, band pull-down (lat activation), banded push-up (increased resistance at top).

*Lower body*: Banded squat (loop band around knees to activate glute medius), banded glute bridge, band hip abduction (clamshell, lateral walk, standing abduction), resistance band deadlift, banded good morning.

*Mobility and warm-up*: Band-assisted stretches, shoulder distraction (shoulder traction stretch for shoulder mobility), hip distraction (band around thigh for hip capsule stretch).

**Resistance levels**: Bands typically range from 5–200+ lb resistance depending on thickness. Beginners use light bands (5–15 lb) for warm-up and activation; advanced trainees use heavy bands (50–200 lb) to add resistance to barbell movements or train muscle groups that lack equipment-based options.

**Limitations**: Resistance cannot be precisely quantified (varies with band length and stretch position), making progressive overload tracking less precise than free weights. Bands wear out over time.

## Pull-Up Bar

The pull-up bar enables the most effective bodyweight vertical pulling exercises, directly targeting the latissimus dorsi, biceps, and scapular muscles.

**Primary pull-up bar exercises:**

*Pulling*: Dead hang, negative pull-up (jump to top, slowly lower), assisted pull-up (band or foot support), pull-up (pronated grip, various widths), chin-up (supinated grip — greater bicep activation), neutral grip pull-up (palms facing each other — easier on wrists and elbows), L-sit pull-up (core activated), weighted pull-up (with weight belt or dumbbell between feet).

*Core and mobility*: Hanging knee raise, hanging leg raise, windshield wiper (advanced), dead hang (shoulder mobility and grip strength).

**Progression from zero pull-ups to multiple**: Week 1–4: Dead hangs (30s) and negative pull-ups (3–5 reps at 3–5 second descent). Week 4–8: Assisted pull-ups (band or foot on chair) + negatives. Week 8–12: First full pull-ups; build to 3×5, then 3×8, then 3×12.

**Grip width effects**: Shoulder-width or just outside shoulder-width is optimal for most lifters — greater lat engagement than very wide grip (which reduces ROM) and better on shoulder joint than extremely narrow grip.

## Kettlebell

Kettlebells are particularly effective for ballistic (explosive) movements, developing power, grip strength, and conditioning simultaneously. The offset center of mass challenges stabiliser muscles differently from dumbbells.

**Primary kettlebell exercises:**

*Ballistic/power*: Kettlebell swing (fundamental; two-hand, one-hand, alternating), kettlebell clean (from floor or from swing), kettlebell snatch (from floor or swing), kettlebell jerk.

*Grinds (strength)*: Goblet squat, Turkish get-up (full-body stability and strength exercise), kettlebell press (overhead, chest press), kettlebell row, kettlebell deadlift, single-leg Romanian deadlift.

*Conditioning*: Kettlebell circuit (swing → goblet squat → press → row → repeat), EMOM protocols, timed sets (e.g., 10 minutes alternating swings).

**Kettlebell swing fundamentals**: Hip hinge (not a squat), explosive hip extension drives the bell, posterior chain (glutes, hamstrings, spinal erectors) is the primary mover. The bell should float momentarily at the top (shoulder height for Russian swing; overhead for American swing). Strict form is required to prevent lower back strain.

## Full Gym

A full gym provides access to all of the above equipment plus machines, cables, and specialised strength equipment. This enables the most varied programming and the greatest specificity of loading.

**Cable machines**: Allow constant tension throughout the full ROM (unlike free weights, which have zero gravity load at certain angles). Particularly effective for isolation exercises: cable fly (chest), cable lateral raise (delts), cable row (back), cable pull-through (glutes and hamstrings), face pull (rear delts and external rotators), cable curl, tricep pushdown.

**Leg press**: Allows heavy quadricep loading without the spinal compression of barbell squats. Useful for training around lower back issues and for volume accumulation.

**Leg curl and leg extension**: Isolation for hamstrings and quadriceps respectively. Machine provides isolation that free weights cannot easily replicate.

**Smith machine**: Guided barbell movement useful for learning squat and press patterns with additional stability, or for training without a spotter (built-in safety hooks). Movement path is fixed and does not allow natural bar path variation.

**Specialty bars**: Trap bar (hex bar) allows a more upright torso during deadlift, reducing lower back stress — excellent for beginners and those with lower back sensitivity. Safety squat bar distributes load forward, accommodating limited shoulder mobility.

**Full gym programming advantages**: Greater exercise variety reduces psychological fatigue of repetitive training, allows greater muscle-specific isolation for hypertrophy, and enables rehabilitation-specific loading patterns unavailable with free weights alone.
