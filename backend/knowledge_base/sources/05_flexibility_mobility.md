# Flexibility, Mobility, and Stretching Science

## Defining Flexibility and Mobility

Flexibility and mobility are related but distinct qualities. **Flexibility** is the passive range of motion (ROM) of a joint or joint system — the maximum range achievable with external assistance or by relaxing the muscles around a joint. **Mobility** is the active, controlled ROM — the range through which a joint can move while maintaining muscular tension and control. Mobility is the more functionally relevant quality for athletic performance and injury prevention.

High flexibility without mobility is of limited functional value: a hypermobile individual who cannot control their range of motion through muscular contraction is more susceptible to injury than a less flexible person with strong control throughout their available range.

## Types of Stretching

**Static stretching**: Holding a stretched position for 15–60 seconds without movement. Effective for increasing passive ROM and reducing post-exercise stiffness. Best performed post-exercise or as a separate flexibility session, not immediately before strength or power training (acute reduction in force production has been documented with pre-exercise static stretching held >45 seconds).

**Dynamic stretching**: Moving through a range of motion repeatedly in a controlled, rhythmic pattern (leg swings, hip circles, arm circles, walking lunges). Elevates tissue temperature, increases synovial fluid production, and activates the muscles through the required ROM. Recommended as part of a pre-exercise warm-up for all training types.

**Proprioceptive Neuromuscular Facilitation (PNF)**: A technique involving a combination of contraction and relaxation to achieve greater ROM than static stretching. The contract-relax method: stretch to end range, hold 6 seconds, relax, stretch further. The contract-relax-antagonist-contract (CRAC) method adds a contraction of the opposing muscle. PNF produces the largest acute ROM gains of any stretching method and is most appropriate for targeted flexibility development work.

**Ballistic stretching**: Bouncing movements at end range of motion. Not recommended for most trainees due to the stretch reflex activation and injury risk. Occasionally used by advanced athletes in sport-specific contexts.

**Foam rolling / self-myofascial release (SMR)**: Applying sustained pressure to soft tissue to reduce myofascial tension and improve tissue quality. Not a true stretching modality but reduces perceived soreness and temporarily improves ROM through neurological mechanisms. Best used before stretching to prepare tissue, and after training for recovery.

## ACSM Flexibility Training Recommendations

ACSM recommends:
- Stretch major muscle groups at least 2–3 days per week, ideally daily
- Hold static stretches for 10–30 seconds for most adults (30–60 seconds for older adults)
- Repeat each stretch 2–4 times for a total of 60 seconds per muscle group
- Perform flexibility training when muscles are warm (post-exercise or after a brief aerobic warm-up)
- Flexibility training should be performed to a point of mild discomfort, not pain

For those with specific flexibility deficits (e.g., tight hip flexors from prolonged sitting, limited shoulder internal rotation), daily targeted stretching of the restricted areas is required to make measurable progress.

## Mobility Work for Strength Training

For resistance training, joint mobility in key patterns is critical for safe and effective execution:

**Hip mobility**: Required for adequate squat depth, deadlift setup, and hip hinge patterns. Common restrictions: hip flexor tightness (from sitting), limited internal rotation, poor adductor flexibility. Targeted work: 90/90 hip stretches, couch stretch (hip flexor), pigeon pose, hip circle mobility drills.

**Thoracic spine mobility**: The thoracic spine (mid-back) must extend and rotate adequately for safe overhead pressing and front rack position. Sedentary individuals often have excessive kyphosis (rounding) and limited thoracic extension. Targeted work: foam roller thoracic extension, thoracic rotation (book openers), cat-cow.

**Ankle dorsiflexion**: Restricted ankle dorsiflexion limits squat depth and forces compensatory forward lean, increasing lower back strain. Target: at least 35° of dorsiflexion with knee over toes. Targeted work: wall ankle mobility drills, calf stretching (soleus and gastrocnemius), heel elevated squats.

**Shoulder mobility**: Overhead pressing and pull-ups require adequate shoulder flexion (180°), external rotation, and scapular control. Restrictions increase impingement risk. Targeted work: doorframe stretches, sleeper stretches (posterior capsule), band pull-aparts.

## Optimal Stretch Duration

**For acute ROM improvement**: Hold static stretches 20–30 seconds; perform 2–4 repetitions. This produces measurable immediate ROM increases and is appropriate for pre-activity dynamic warm-up supplemented with brief static stretching of restricted areas.

**For chronic flexibility improvement**: 30–60 second holds, 4–6 repetitions, 5–7 days per week over 4–6 weeks. Consistent daily stretching is required to produce lasting structural changes in connective tissue viscoelasticity and musculotendinous unit length.

**For PNF**: 6-second maximal isometric contraction followed by 30-second passive stretch, repeated 3–4 times. Most time-efficient method for rapid flexibility development.

## Timing of Flexibility Work

**Pre-exercise**: Dynamic stretching and mobility drills only. Static stretching lasting more than 45 seconds may reduce force production by 5–8% acutely. A 5–10 minute dynamic warm-up with movement-specific mobility work is optimal preparation.

**Post-exercise**: Ideal time for static stretching and PNF. Muscles are warm and more extensible, and the stretching aids in reducing post-exercise muscle tension. The evidence that post-exercise stretching prevents DOMS (delayed onset muscle soreness) is weak, but it improves ROM and is appropriate at this time.

**Standalone flexibility sessions**: Any time of day, though warm muscles respond better. A brief 5-minute aerobic warm-up before a dedicated flexibility session improves outcomes.

## Flexibility Considerations for the Increase-Flexibility Goal

Trainees with an explicit flexibility goal should:
- Perform dedicated flexibility sessions 4–5 times per week (not just post-workout add-ons)
- Include yoga, Pilates, or dedicated mobility work as primary training modalities
- Prioritise the joints most limiting daily function or sport performance
- Use PNF techniques for the most restricted areas
- Allow 8–12 weeks for meaningful structural flexibility improvements (neurological adaptations produce faster early gains; structural changes take longer)
- Pair flexibility work with strength training of the newly acquired ROM to build active control (stability in hypermobility)
