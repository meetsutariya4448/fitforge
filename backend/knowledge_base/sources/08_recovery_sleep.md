# Recovery, Sleep, and Adaptation Science

## Supercompensation Theory

Supercompensation is the physiological model that explains how training leads to improved fitness. The model has four phases:

1. **Training stimulus**: A training session imposes stress that temporarily reduces performance capacity (fatigue phase).
2. **Recovery**: After the session, the body repairs damaged tissue and restores energy substrates.
3. **Supercompensation**: Given adequate recovery time, the body adapts above the pre-training baseline — the muscles, tendons, cardiovascular system, and nervous system become slightly more capable than before.
4. **Detraining**: If the next training stimulus does not arrive during the supercompensation window, fitness returns to baseline.

Effective training programs time the next session to arrive during the supercompensation window — not too soon (before recovery is complete), not too late (after fitness has returned to baseline). For most resistance training stimuli, the supercompensation window is approximately 48–96 hours post-session, which is why training a muscle group 2–3 times per week is optimal for most trainees.

## Sleep and Athletic Performance

Sleep is the most anabolic activity available to a trainee. The majority of muscle protein synthesis and growth hormone release occur during slow-wave (deep) and REM sleep. Inadequate sleep directly impairs training adaptation and performance.

**Key research findings:**

- Sleep restriction to 6 hours per night for 14 days impairs cognitive performance as severely as 24 hours of total sleep deprivation, but subjects do not perceive themselves as impaired (Dinges et al., 2005).
- Athletes sleeping 10 hours per night vs 6 hours show measurable improvements in sprint times, reaction time, and shooting accuracy within 2 weeks (Mah et al., 2011).
- A single night of 4-hour sleep reduces testosterone by 10–15% in young men (Leproult & Van Cauter, 2011).
- Cortisol (catabolic hormone that promotes muscle breakdown) rises significantly with sleep restriction.

**Recommendations for athletes**: 7–9 hours per night for adults, 8–10 hours for adolescents and those with heavy training loads. Consistent sleep timing (same bed and wake time) is nearly as important as duration.

## Growth Hormone and Sleep

Growth hormone (GH) is secreted in pulses during slow-wave sleep, particularly in the first sleep cycle (approximately 90 minutes after sleep onset). GH stimulates protein synthesis, fat oxidation, and tissue repair. A single night of disrupted sleep significantly reduces GH secretion.

Practices that support GH release during sleep:
- Consistent sleep schedule (irregular bedtimes disrupt slow-wave sleep cycles)
- Dark, cool room (18–20°C optimal for sleep quality)
- No alcohol (suppresses REM and deep sleep even in small amounts)
- No large meals within 2–3 hours of sleep (elevated insulin suppresses GH)
- Minimise blue-light exposure 1–2 hours before bed (suppresses melatonin)

## Heart Rate Variability (HRV) as a Recovery Metric

HRV is the variation in time between consecutive heartbeats. High HRV indicates the autonomic nervous system is well-recovered and parasympathetic tone (rest-and-digest) is dominant. Low HRV indicates sympathetic dominance (fight-or-flight), a sign of incomplete recovery, high stress, or illness.

HRV is measured most accurately in the morning immediately upon waking, before rising. Many wearables (Garmin, WHOOP, Apple Watch with third-party apps) provide daily HRV readings. Trends over 2–4 weeks are more meaningful than single readings.

**Practical use of HRV:**
- Significantly elevated HRV (>10–15% above 7-day average): consider adding intensity; the body is well-recovered
- Slightly depressed HRV (5–10% below average): train normally or reduce intensity
- Significantly depressed HRV (>15% below average): rest or active recovery only; something is impairing recovery (illness, alcohol, high stress, poor sleep)

HRV-guided training (increasing or decreasing intensity based on daily HRV readings) has been shown to produce similar or greater fitness gains with lower total training stress compared to fixed-program training.

## Active Recovery Methods

Active recovery — low-intensity movement performed on rest days or between intense sessions — accelerates recovery vs complete rest by:
- Increasing blood flow to trained muscles, enhancing nutrient delivery and waste removal
- Maintaining neuromuscular activation patterns
- Reducing perceived stiffness and soreness

**Effective active recovery modalities:**
- Walking (30–60 minutes at a comfortable pace)
- Light cycling (Zone 1, 30–45 minutes, 60–70% HRmax)
- Swimming (particularly effective for full-body recovery and mobility)
- Yoga or light mobility work (combined benefits for flexibility and stress reduction)
- Foam rolling (10–15 minutes targeting trained muscle groups)
- Low-intensity rowing (non-impact, full-body circulation)

Active recovery should feel genuinely easy — RPE 3–4/10 maximum. If it feels challenging, it is compromising recovery rather than aiding it.

## Signs of Overtraining Syndrome

Overtraining syndrome (OTS) results from accumulated training stress without adequate recovery and is distinct from normal training fatigue. True OTS can require weeks to months to resolve.

**Signs of functional overreaching (early, reversible with 1–2 weeks rest):**
- Declining performance in sessions despite adequate effort
- Elevated resting heart rate (>5 bpm above baseline)
- Increased perceived exertion at a fixed workload
- Persistent muscle soreness beyond 72 hours
- Reduced motivation for training

**Signs of non-functional overreaching or OTS (requires longer recovery):**
- Performance decline lasting >2–3 weeks
- Mood disturbance: irritability, depression, anxiety
- Persistent fatigue that sleep does not resolve
- Increased illness frequency (immune suppression)
- Significant HRV depression over multiple weeks
- Hormonal disruption (reduced testosterone, elevated cortisol, disrupted menstrual cycle in female athletes)

**Prevention**: Planned deload weeks every 4–8 weeks, adequate sleep, adequate caloric intake relative to training load, and monitoring HRV and subjective wellbeing scores.

## Nutrition Timing for Recovery

Post-exercise nutrition timing influences recovery speed and muscle protein synthesis rates:

**Protein post-exercise**: 20–40 g high-quality protein (leucine-rich: whey, egg, chicken, fish) within 2 hours of training maximises muscle protein synthesis. The anabolic window is larger than traditionally thought (2–3 hours rather than 30 minutes), but post-exercise protein intake is still important.

**Carbohydrates post-exercise**: Carbohydrates restore muscle glycogen depleted during training. Important after long or high-intensity sessions; less critical after short strength sessions. 1–1.5 g/kg body weight within 2 hours speeds glycogen restoration for those training again within 8–24 hours.

**Fluids and electrolytes**: Replace fluid lost during exercise (0.5–1 L per hour of training is typical). Electrolytes (sodium, potassium, magnesium) are lost in sweat and must be replaced, particularly after sessions lasting >60 minutes or in hot environments.

## Psychological Recovery and Mental Fatigue

Training adaptation requires not just physical recovery but psychological recovery. Mental fatigue — from cognitive work, stress, poor sleep, or prolonged training monotony — impairs exercise performance and reduces training motivation.

Strategies for psychological recovery:
- Schedule dedicated rest days with no training-related activity
- Include activities that promote relaxation and enjoyment outside of training
- Vary training modalities to prevent monotony
- Address life stress as part of the recovery picture (work, relationship, and financial stress all impair recovery independently of training load)
- Use RPE and self-reported motivation as inputs to training load decisions alongside objective metrics like HRV
