# Nutrition Fundamentals for Fitness

## Protein: Requirements, Timing, and Sources

Protein is the most critical macronutrient for anyone training seriously, regardless of goal. It provides the amino acids necessary for muscle repair, muscle protein synthesis, immune function, enzyme production, and hormone synthesis.

**Requirements by goal:**

- **General health (sedentary to lightly active)**: 0.8 g/kg body weight per day (RDA). Adequate for tissue maintenance but insufficient to support meaningful muscle growth or preservation during fat loss.
- **Muscle building (active resistance trainees)**: 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day. The upper end of this range maximises muscle protein synthesis; further increases above 2.2 g/kg produce diminishing returns in most individuals.
- **Fat loss with muscle preservation**: 2.0–3.1 g/kg/day. Higher protein intakes during caloric deficit better protect lean mass from catabolism and increase satiety.
- **Older adults (50+)**: 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day minimum due to reduced anabolic sensitivity (anabolic resistance — higher protein doses are required to stimulate the same muscle protein synthesis response).

**Protein distribution**: Recent research (Areta et al., 2013) shows that distributing protein evenly across 3–4 meals (25–40 g per meal) maximises daily muscle protein synthesis compared to uneven distribution (one large protein feeding or many small ones). The anabolic ceiling per meal is approximately 40 g for most individuals, after which excess amino acids are oxidised for energy.

**High-quality protein sources**: Whey protein (fast-absorbing, high leucine — ideal post-exercise), casein (slow-absorbing — ideal before sleep), chicken breast, lean beef, eggs (whole eggs provide cholesterol for hormone synthesis), fish (salmon also provides omega-3 fatty acids), Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, legumes (incomplete protein — pair with grains for complete amino acid profile), tofu and tempeh.

**Leucine threshold**: Leucine is the primary amino acid triggering muscle protein synthesis. A minimum of 2–3 g leucine per meal is required to maximally stimulate synthesis. Whey protein, eggs, and chicken all provide adequate leucine at 25–30 g protein per serving; plant sources may require larger servings to reach threshold.

## Carbohydrates: Fuel, Timing, and Role in Training

Carbohydrates are the primary fuel source for high-intensity exercise. Muscle glycogen (stored carbohydrate in muscle tissue) is the predominant energy substrate during resistance training and aerobic exercise above 65% HRmax.

**Carbohydrate requirements**: Vary by training volume and intensity. Sedentary individuals need minimal carbohydrate beyond what is needed for brain function. Endurance athletes may need 5–10 g/kg/day; strength athletes 3–5 g/kg/day; general fitness trainees 2–4 g/kg/day.

**Glycaemic index and training**: During the hour before and after intense training, higher-glycaemic carbohydrates (rice, potatoes, oats, fruit) rapidly restore glycogen. Outside of training windows, lower-glycaemic sources (vegetables, legumes, whole grains) provide sustained energy and better satiety.

**Carbohydrate periodisation**: Matching carbohydrate intake to training demands. High-carbohydrate days on heavy training days; lower-carbohydrate days on rest days or low-intensity sessions. This approach optimises glycogen availability when needed while reducing total carbohydrate intake on days where it is not required.

**Common myth**: Very low-carbohydrate diets (ketogenic) can maintain muscle mass and performance in the short term, but research consistently shows that carbohydrate availability improves high-intensity exercise performance. Ketogenic diets are not optimal for maximal strength or hypertrophy goals in most individuals.

## Fats: Hormones, Joint Health, and Fat-Soluble Vitamins

Dietary fat is essential for:
- Hormone production (testosterone, oestrogen, cortisol are cholesterol-derived)
- Absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K)
- Cellular membrane integrity
- Joint lubrication (synovial fluid composition)
- Anti-inflammatory function (omega-3 fatty acids)

**Omega-3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA)**: Found in fatty fish (salmon, sardines, mackerel), flaxseed, and walnuts. Reduce inflammation, support cardiovascular health, and may modestly increase muscle protein synthesis (research ongoing). Target: 1–3 g EPA+DHA per day, achievable through 2–3 fatty fish servings per week or fish oil supplementation.

**Fat requirements**: Minimum 20–35% of total calories from fat to maintain hormone production. Dropping below 15% of calories from fat can reduce testosterone in men and disrupt menstrual function in women. Dietary fat is not the direct cause of body fat accumulation — caloric surplus is.

## Pre- and Post-Exercise Nutrition

**Pre-exercise (2–3 hours before)**: A mixed meal of protein (20–40 g), carbohydrates (0.5–1 g/kg), and low fat is ideal. Fat and fibre slow gastric emptying and may cause GI discomfort during intense exercise — minimise in the final hour before training.

**If training within 60 minutes**: A small, easy-to-digest snack: banana + protein shake, or rice cakes + peanut butter + honey. Prioritise rapidly-digested carbohydrates for immediate energy.

**Post-exercise (within 2 hours)**: 20–40 g protein + 1–1.5 g/kg carbohydrates. The protein stimulates muscle protein synthesis; the carbohydrates restore glycogen. A practical example: chicken breast + rice + vegetables, or Greek yogurt + fruit + granola, or protein shake + banana.

## Hydration and Performance

Even mild dehydration (1–2% of body weight) measurably impairs strength, power, endurance, and cognitive function. A 2% deficit (1.4 kg in a 70 kg individual) reduces muscular endurance by 10–15%.

**Daily hydration target**: 35–45 ml/kg body weight (approximately 2.5–3.5 L/day for a 70–80 kg individual), adjusted upward for sweat losses during training.

**During exercise**: 400–800 ml of water per hour of training. In sessions lasting >60 minutes, electrolyte replacement (sodium, potassium) improves fluid retention and prevents hyponatraemia.

**Urine colour as hydration indicator**: Pale yellow = well hydrated; dark yellow = mild dehydration; amber = significant dehydration requiring immediate fluid intake.

## Key Supplements with Research Support

**Creatine monohydrate**: The most extensively researched ergogenic supplement. Increases phosphocreatine stores in muscle, enhancing ATP regeneration for high-intensity efforts. 3–5 g/day produces maximal saturation within 3–4 weeks. Benefits: increased strength (5–10%), increased muscle mass (1–2 kg lean mass in first month), improved recovery. No adverse effects in healthy individuals. No loading phase required.

**Caffeine**: 3–6 mg/kg consumed 30–60 minutes before training improves strength, endurance, and pain tolerance. Individual response varies (CYP1A2 gene affects metabolism speed). Avoid consuming within 6–8 hours of sleep.

**Vitamin D**: Many individuals are deficient (particularly in northern climates or those with limited sun exposure). Supports testosterone production, immune function, and bone health. 1,000–4,000 IU/day is a common supplementation target; blood level testing provides a precise recommendation.

**Protein supplements (whey, casein, plant-based)**: Convenient way to meet protein targets, not a replacement for whole food protein sources. No advantage over food protein for muscle protein synthesis; simply a practical tool.

**Supplements with insufficient evidence**: BCAAs (redundant with adequate dietary protein), glutamine (redundant with adequate protein), most pre-workout stimulant blends beyond caffeine's documented effects.
