# Weight Loss and Fat Loss Principles

## Energy Balance and Caloric Deficit

Fat loss requires a sustained caloric deficit: consuming fewer calories than the body expends over time. One kilogram of body fat contains approximately 7,700 kcal of stored energy. A deficit of 500–750 kcal per day produces roughly 0.5–0.75 kg of fat loss per week — a rate consistent with ACSM recommendations for sustainable loss. Deficits larger than 1,000 kcal/day increase the risk of lean mass loss, micronutrient deficiencies, hormonal disruption, and unsustainable hunger.

Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) has four components:
- **Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR)**: Calories burned at rest (~60–70% of TDEE)
- **Thermic Effect of Food (TEF)**: Energy cost of digesting food (~10% of TDEE)
- **Exercise Activity Thermogenesis (EAT)**: Intentional exercise (~10–20% of TDEE)
- **Non-Exercise Activity Thermogenesis (NEAT)**: All movement outside formal exercise (fidgeting, walking, standing) — highly variable and the most responsive component to changes in energy availability

NEAT is critical to fat loss: studies show NEAT can vary by up to 2,000 kcal/day between individuals of similar size. It also drops significantly when calories are restricted, partially compensating for the deficit. Deliberately maintaining or increasing NEAT (walking more, using stairs) during a fat-loss phase improves results.

## Optimal Caloric Deficit Rate

ACSM and major nutrition bodies recommend losing 0.5–1% of body weight per week as the upper limit for fat loss while preserving lean mass. For a 90 kg individual, this is 0.45–0.9 kg per week. Faster rates of loss are associated with greater lean mass loss, particularly without adequate protein intake and resistance training.

A modest deficit of 300–500 kcal/day is more sustainable than aggressive restriction. Compliance over months matters more than perfection over weeks.

## The Role of Protein in Fat Loss

Protein intake is the most important dietary variable during fat loss, serving three functions: muscle preservation, satiety, and thermic effect.

**Muscle preservation**: In a caloric deficit, the body catabolises lean tissue for energy. Higher protein intake (1.6–2.4 g/kg body weight, or even higher during aggressive restriction) signals muscle protein synthesis and reduces muscle breakdown. Research suggests active individuals in a deficit may benefit from 2.2–3.1 g/kg to maximally protect lean mass.

**Satiety**: Protein is the most satiating macronutrient per calorie, reducing appetite and spontaneous caloric intake. High-protein diets produce greater adherence to deficit-based eating due to reduced hunger.

**Thermic effect**: Protein has a thermic effect of 20–30% (vs 5–10% for carbohydrates and 0–3% for fat), meaning more calories from protein are burned in digestion.

Practical targets: prioritise lean protein sources at every meal — chicken, fish, eggs, dairy, legumes, tofu. Minimum 25–40 g protein per meal for maximal muscle protein synthesis stimulation.

## LISS vs HIIT for Fat Loss

Both Low-Intensity Steady-State (LISS) and High-Intensity Interval Training (HIIT) produce fat loss when combined with appropriate caloric deficit. Their trade-offs differ:

**LISS (Zone 2, 60–70% HRmax, 30–60+ minutes)**:
- Burns a higher percentage of calories from fat during exercise (fat oxidation is dominant at low intensities)
- Lower recovery cost, can be performed on any day
- Does not interfere significantly with resistance training recovery
- Accumulates more total activity time per week easily
- Appropriate for beginners, those with joint issues, and as active recovery

**HIIT (>80% HRmax, 15–30 minutes including rest)**:
- Burns fewer calories during the session than LISS of equal duration, but more total daily energy (EPOC effect)
- Greater EPOC (Excess Post-exercise Oxygen Consumption): elevated metabolic rate for 12–24 hours post-session
- Time-efficient
- Higher recovery cost; may interfere with resistance training if scheduled the same day
- Not suitable as the primary cardio modality for beginners; should be introduced gradually

For most fat-loss programs, 2–3 sessions of moderate-intensity cardio (LISS or Zone 3) per week combined with 3–4 resistance training sessions produces the best body composition outcomes.

## Excess Post-Exercise Oxygen Consumption (EPOC)

EPOC refers to the elevated oxygen consumption (and corresponding calorie burn) that persists after exercise as the body restores homeostasis. High-intensity exercise and resistance training produce larger and longer EPOC effects than low-intensity steady-state cardio. However, EPOC from a typical HIIT session adds only 50–150 extra calories — meaningful but not dramatic. The primary benefit of HIIT for fat loss is its time efficiency and cardiovascular fitness improvement, not dramatically higher caloric burn.

## Body Recomposition

Body recomposition — simultaneously losing fat and gaining muscle — is possible under specific conditions: training beginners, those returning after a long layoff, or individuals at higher body fat percentages consuming adequate protein. For experienced, lean trainees, simultaneous fat loss and muscle gain is very difficult and typically requires precise caloric management (cycling between small deficit and maintenance phases).

The most effective recomposition approach for eligible individuals: eat at or near maintenance calories (±100–200 kcal), consume 1.8–2.4 g/kg protein, and perform a structured resistance training program. The body preferentially uses stored fat for energy during a slight deficit while building muscle from dietary protein and training stimulus.

## Metabolism and Adaptive Thermogenesis

Prolonged caloric restriction causes adaptive thermogenesis: the body reduces BMR by 10–15% beyond what would be expected from the loss of lean mass alone. This is a metabolic adaptation that evolved to conserve energy during periods of food scarcity. It partially accounts for the phenomenon known as "metabolic damage" in heavily-dieted individuals, though the effect is reversible when calories are returned to maintenance.

Diet breaks (2 weeks at maintenance during a fat-loss phase) and refeeds (1–2 days at maintenance per week) partially offset adaptive thermogenesis and improve long-term fat loss outcomes by maintaining hormones like leptin and thyroid hormones that regulate metabolism.

## Common Fat Loss Mistakes

**Cardio without resistance training**: Fat loss without resistance training typically results in significant muscle loss along with fat loss, leading to a lower BMR and a "skinny fat" body composition.

**Excessive restriction**: Deficits over 1,000 kcal/day accelerate muscle loss, worsen hormonal adaptation, impair recovery from training, and are unsustainable.

**Neglecting NEAT**: Adding formal exercise while inadvertently reducing daily movement (sitting more, driving instead of walking) blunts the caloric deficit.

**Chasing the scale short-term**: Body weight fluctuates 1–3 kg daily based on water, food volume, hormones, and sodium intake. Weekly averages and body measurements are more reliable progress indicators than daily weigh-ins.

**Not tracking food accurately**: Studies consistently show people underestimate caloric intake by 20–50% without measuring. Tracking food intake, even imperfectly, dramatically improves accuracy and results.
