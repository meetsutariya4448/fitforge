# Cardiovascular and Endurance Training Guidelines

## ACSM Cardiorespiratory Exercise Recommendations

The ACSM recommends that adults engage in at least 150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity cardiorespiratory exercise, or 75 minutes per week of vigorous-intensity exercise, or an equivalent combination. These thresholds represent the minimum for general health maintenance. For meaningful improvements in cardiovascular fitness (VO2max, cardiac output, mitochondrial density), more structured and progressive programming is required.

Cardiorespiratory fitness is measured by VO2max — the maximum rate of oxygen consumption during maximal exercise. A higher VO2max is one of the strongest predictors of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular health across all age groups. VO2max improves in response to endurance training that systematically stresses the oxygen delivery and utilisation systems.

## The FITT Principle for Endurance Training

The FITT principle provides the framework for endurance programming:

**Frequency**: 3–5 days per week for cardiorespiratory improvement. Fewer than 3 days per week is insufficient stimulus for VO2max gains. More than 5 days per week at moderate-to-high intensity increases overuse injury risk without proportional fitness benefit for most trainees.

**Intensity**: The primary driver of cardiovascular adaptation. Intensity is prescribed using heart rate zones, percentage of VO2max, or RPE (Rate of Perceived Exertion):
- Zone 1 (recovery): <65% HRmax, RPE 2–3/10. Enhances recovery, builds aerobic base.
- Zone 2 (aerobic base): 65–75% HRmax, RPE 4–5/10. Primary zone for building mitochondrial density and fat oxidation capacity. Forms 70–80% of total training volume in polarised models.
- Zone 3 (tempo): 75–85% HRmax, RPE 6–7/10. Improves lactate threshold. Moderately hard; can be sustained for 20–60 minutes.
- Zone 4 (threshold): 85–92% HRmax, RPE 7–8/10. Just below or at lactate threshold. High physiological benefit, high fatigue cost.
- Zone 5 (VO2max): >92% HRmax, RPE 9–10/10. Maximal aerobic intervals lasting 30 seconds to 4 minutes. Highest VO2max stimulus.

**Time**: Session duration depends on intensity. Low-intensity sessions can last 45–90+ minutes. High-intensity interval sessions last 20–40 minutes including warm-up and cool-down. The target is 150–300 minutes per week of moderate-intensity or 75–150 minutes of vigorous-intensity exercise.

**Type**: Any activity that rhythmically engages large muscle groups is appropriate — running, cycling, swimming, rowing, elliptical, hiking. The best modality is the one the individual will sustain. Varying modalities reduces overuse injury risk.

## Heart Rate Training Zones

Maximum heart rate (HRmax) is commonly estimated as `220 − age`, though this formula has a standard deviation of ±10–12 bpm and should be verified by a maximal effort test where possible. More accurate estimates use the Tanaka formula: `208 − (0.7 × age)`.

Heart rate reserve (HRR) method for training zone prescription (Karvonen formula):
```
Target HR = ((HRmax − HRrest) × intensity %) + HRrest
```
This accounts for resting heart rate and gives more individualised zones.

Training with a heart rate monitor or wearable provides real-time intensity feedback, reducing the risk of training too hard on aerobic base days and not hard enough on high-intensity days.

## VO2max Improvement: Key Training Strategies

**High-Intensity Interval Training (HIIT)**: Short intervals at ≥90% VO2max with incomplete recovery. Classic protocols include 4×4 minutes at 90–95% HRmax with 3-minute recovery intervals (Norwegian 4×4), and 30 seconds at maximal effort with 4-minute recovery (Wingate protocol). HIIT produces the largest acute VO2max stimulus and is time-efficient.

**Tempo runs / lactate threshold training**: Sustained effort at 80–85% HRmax for 20–40 minutes. Shifts the lactate threshold — the speed or power output at which lactate begins accumulating faster than it is cleared — upward, allowing the athlete to sustain higher intensities.

**Long slow distance (LSD)**: Extended sessions at Zone 2 intensity. Increases mitochondrial density, capillary density, and cardiac stroke volume over many weeks. Essential for aerobic base development.

**Polarised training model**: Research (Seiler, 2010) on elite endurance athletes shows that approximately 80% of training volume at low intensity (Zones 1–2) and 20% at high intensity (Zones 4–5), with minimal time in Zone 3, produces superior endurance adaptations compared to training concentrated in Zone 3. The pyramid model (high volume at low intensity, less at moderate, least at high) follows similar principles.

## RPE Scales for Intensity Prescription

**Borg RPE Scale (6–20)**: Correlates directly with heart rate (multiply by 10 for approximate HR). Moderate intensity = 12–14; vigorous = 15–17.

**Modified Borg CR10 Scale (0–10)**: Easier for most athletes to use. Moderate intensity = 4–5; vigorous = 6–7; maximal = 9–10.

**Talk test**: A practical field method. Moderate intensity: can speak in full sentences. Vigorous intensity: can only speak in short phrases. Maximal: cannot speak comfortably.

## Cardiovascular Adaptations to Endurance Training

Consistent endurance training produces the following adaptations over 8–12 weeks:

**Cardiac adaptations**: Increased stroke volume (more blood ejected per heartbeat), lower resting heart rate, and larger left ventricular end-diastolic volume (eccentric hypertrophy). Elite endurance athletes often have resting heart rates of 35–45 bpm and cardiac outputs 30–50% above sedentary individuals.

**Peripheral adaptations**: Increased capillary density in trained muscles improves oxygen delivery. Increased mitochondrial density and oxidative enzyme activity improve oxygen utilisation efficiency.

**Metabolic adaptations**: Greater fat oxidation at a given workload (sparing glycogen), increased lactate threshold, improved muscle glycogen storage, enhanced glucose uptake after exercise.

**VO2max improvement**: Typically 10–30% improvement in previously sedentary individuals over 12–20 weeks of structured training. Highly trained individuals see smaller relative gains but at higher absolute levels.

## Endurance Training for Beginners

Beginners should start with 2–3 sessions per week of low-to-moderate intensity cardio (Zone 1–2), building session duration by no more than 10% per week (the 10% rule) to reduce overuse injury risk. Walking, cycling, and swimming are appropriate entry points for those with joint sensitivity. Running beginners benefit from run-walk intervals (e.g., 2 minutes running, 2 minutes walking) that allow adaptation while managing musculoskeletal stress.

The first 4–8 weeks of endurance training produce rapid neural and cardiovascular adaptations even at low intensities. Beginners do not need high-intensity work to see significant fitness improvements.

## Concurrent Training: Endurance and Resistance Combined

When combining endurance and resistance training (concurrent training), ACSM recommends performing resistance training before endurance training in the same session where possible, or separating the two by at least 6 hours. Endurance work immediately before resistance training impairs maximal force production. The interference effect — where endurance training blunts hypertrophy from resistance training — is minimised by keeping endurance sessions at low-to-moderate intensity and moderate duration (<45 minutes per session).

## Monitoring Progress in Endurance Training

Common metrics for tracking endurance improvement:
- Resting heart rate (decreases with improved aerobic fitness)
- Heart rate at a fixed workload (decreases as efficiency improves)
- Pace or power at a fixed heart rate (improves as fitness develops)
- Time to complete a fixed distance
- Estimated VO2max from wearable devices (useful for trend tracking, less accurate in absolute terms)
